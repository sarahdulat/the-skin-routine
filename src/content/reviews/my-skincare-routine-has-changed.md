## ✨ A lot of you have been asking about my skincare routine ✨

Hello, my name is Sarah, and skincare is one of those subjects I can discuss for an unreasonable amount of time. Most people who ask about my routine are genuinely curious. They want the products, the order, the instructions, and ideally some sort of guarantee that if they do exactly what I do, they will wake up looking ten years younger. Then I start explaining my actual routine and somewhere around step seven their eyes get a little glassy.

“You are going to have to write this down for me.”

So, eventually, I did.

This blog began after many versions of that exact conversation. I first put my routine into a Google Sheet with every step, instructions, links, and alternatives for different skin types and concerns. I thought this was extremely helpful and comprehensive. The immediate response was, “What if I’m not as dedicated as you?” and, “What if I have a smaller budget?” Fair questions. An eleven-step routine is not for everyone, and skincare advice is only useful if it fits into your actual life.

That made me want to create something more interactive: a place where I could share not only my own routine, but also realistic alternatives based on things like time, budget, skin type, and specific concerns. I am not a dermatologist or a certified aesthetician, and I will never pretend to be one. I am simply the person in my circle whom everyone asks about skincare. I have spent years reading, comparing, experimenting, paying attention to my skin, and staying very consistent. My friends and family encouraged me to do something useful with that experience. Some of them also keep telling me to create my own skincare line, but that sounds majorly daunting, so for now we are sticking with the blog. 😅

My perspective is also inevitably my own. I am a cis white woman of European descent, and my skin is not everyone’s skin. I have dealt with hormonal cystic acne, high testosterone levels, changing hormones, pregnancy, travel, dry winters, humid summers, and the logistical problem of loving a product that is sold on the wrong continent. I can tell you what has worked for me, explain why I chose it, and research alternatives, but no routine is universal. If you have a persistent skin condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are unsure about an active ingredient, please speak with a dermatologist or your healthcare provider.

With that important disclaimer out of the way, I would like to talk about how my routine has evolved—because it has changed quite a lot since I first became truly serious about skincare in April 2018.

The products have changed. The industry has changed. My address changed. My hormones changed. My available time _definitely_ changed after becoming a mother. But the structure and philosophy of my routine have remained surprisingly stable. The products change, but the steps persist.

## The Routine That Started in 2018

I took care of my skin before 2018. I started using eye cream at 26, which felt very responsible at the time. But April 2018 is when I began the routine that, more or less, became the foundation of what I still do today.

It was involved. I enjoyed that. I liked knowing what every step was meant to accomplish, and I was willing to spend the time doing it. The routine was not an occasional act of self-care reserved for a Sunday evening with a candle. It was daily maintenance. Morning and night. At home and while traveling. When I felt motivated and when I absolutely did not. (Can you guess my zodiac sign? If you guessed Virgo you'd be right.)

That consistency changed my skin more than any one “miracle” product ever could. Over time, I was able to stop wearing face makeup and use only eye makeup when I felt like it. This does not mean I achieved poreless, airbrushed, doll skin, because that is only achievable with filters. It means I became comfortable with my skin as it actually looked.

For transparency, I do not use filters on photos of myself or get regular facials or aesthetic treatments. I do not use Botox or fillers, except Botox for TMJ in the past, although I would consider any of those things in the future. Genetics may help, but I know what my skin was beginning to look like at 31 and what changed when I became consistent. My very scientific game of “guess my age” suggests I often appear younger than I am—the lowest guess was 24 😱—but looking younger is not the only goal. I want healthy, comfortable, resilient, protected skin that ages well, not skin that somehow avoids aging altogether.

## When Every Ingredient Had Its Own Bottle

If you became interested in skincare around the same time I did, you may remember when bathroom counters started looking like chemistry labs. We had a separate bottle for hyaluronic acid, another for niacinamide, another for peptides, and perhaps several more that required a diagram to layer correctly. The Ordinary deserves a lot of credit for making this approach popular.

The brand made individual ingredients visible, affordable, and understandable. Instead of buying a mysterious cream with vague claims about “radiance,” people began learning what niacinamide does, why hyaluronic acid can help support hydration, and how different active ingredients fit into a routine. It encouraged us to read labels and think critically about what we were putting on our skin. That education was incredibly valuable.

It was also very easy to get carried away.

Once you understand that several ingredients can be useful, the natural impulse is to buy all of them. Then you stand in front of the mirror applying one watery serum after another, waiting between layers and hoping nothing pills when you finally reach sunscreen. You have technically created a personalized formula, but you have also created a part-time job.

My earlier routine contained many of these single-ingredient serums. At that time, it made sense. If I wanted niacinamide, peptides, and hyaluronic acid, separate products were often the clearest and most affordable way to get them. It also helped me isolate what my skin liked and what it did not. I could introduce one product at a time and pay attention to the result.

Today, I use far fewer of those bottles—not because the ingredients stopped being useful, but because skincare formulations improved. Niacinamide, peptides, and hyaluronic acid have become common components of well-formulated hydrating serums and moisturizers. In many cases, they are no longer the headline; they are the baseline. I expect a good hydrating product to do more than one thing.

If one serum already gives me hydration, barrier support, and several helpful ingredients in a formula designed to work together, I do not need to apply three additional serums simply for the satisfaction of completing more steps. I can save the extra time for a targeted active that actually adds something different—or, as a mother, for the approximately 400 other things competing for those minutes.

This has been one of the biggest philosophical changes in my routine. I used to think of customization as adding another product. Now I think of it as choosing a smarter product and removing the unnecessary ones.

That does not mean single-ingredient serums are bad or obsolete. They are still useful when you want to introduce an ingredient carefully, control its concentration, identify the cause of a reaction, or address a very specific concern. They also remain an accessible way to learn about skincare. But most people do not need every trending ingredient in its own dropper bottle, and more layers do not automatically produce better skin.

I still love a routine. I am still dedicated. I have simply become less interested in steps for the sake of steps.

## Pregnancy Made Me Read Every Label Again

Pregnancy changed my relationship with my routine in a very practical way: products I had used without thinking were suddenly products I needed to reconsider.

Retinoids were the most obvious category. I stopped using retinol during pregnancy, following the standard medical guidance to avoid topical retinoids, and reviewed the rest of my products for ingredients that were not recommended or that I did not feel comfortable using. This was not the moment for a casual “it is probably fine.” I discussed questions with my healthcare providers and chose a cautious approach.

Going through the entire routine was illuminating. When a routine has grown slowly over several years, each individual addition can seem reasonable. It is only when you examine everything at once that you realize how much you are using. Pregnancy forced me to ask: What is essential? What is redundant? What is here because it is helping, and what is here because I am accustomed to applying it?

I simplified.

I focused on gentle cleansing, hydration, supporting my skin barrier, and mineral sun protection. I looked for pregnancy-compatible ways to manage my concerns and accepted that this was not the season for every possible active ingredient. My goal was not to find a loophole that would let me recreate my exact pre-pregnancy routine. It was to take good care of my skin within a new set of boundaries.

My hormones also changed. Before pregnancy, I had struggled with hormonal cystic acne caused by high testosterone levels, which I managed with birth control and Spironolactone under medical care. Pregnancy seemed to reset my hormones, and I am no longer taking medication for those issues. I do not present this as a treatment recommendation or a predictable pregnancy benefit—it is simply what happened in my body. Hormonal skin changes are deeply individual, and pregnancy can make acne better, worse, or merely different.

After pregnancy, I did not immediately rebuild the old routine bottle by bottle. Motherhood had changed the context. My available time, energy, sleep, and attention were different. I still cared about my skin and still believed in consistency, but the routine needed to remain realistic enough that I could actually do it.

That experience made me better at separating a routine’s core from its extras. Cleanse appropriately. Treat the concerns that matter. Moisturize. Use SPF every day. Everything else has to justify the time, cost, and space it takes up.

## Then I Moved to Europe

Moving to Europe changed my routine again, this time because geography apparently likes to have opinions about skincare.

I live in Switzerland, very close to the German and French borders. This gives me access to three distinct shopping environments, which is both excellent and dangerous. I can visit French pharmacies, German drugstores, and Swiss retailers without much trouble. I also visit family in Poland often, so Polish skincare brands have gradually entered the rotation as well.

What I cannot always do is casually replace every American product I used to buy.

Some US products are unavailable here. Others technically ship to Switzerland but become absurd once international shipping, import fees, or retailer markups are involved. And some products cost noticeably more even when they are easy to find. There are few things more irritating than paying substantially more for the exact same bottle simply because it crossed an ocean.

At first, I kept a mental list for visiting friends and family. If someone was coming from the US and had suitcase space, they might receive a very specific skincare request. I still do this for a few products that I genuinely love and cannot replace. If a product performs reliably and does not irritate my skin, I am not going to abandon it just to make the routine look more convenient to obtain.

But relying on international skincare couriers is not a complete strategy. Eventually, I needed replacements that were easy to source locally and did not make me angry every time I saw the price.

This turned out to be a gift.

## The French Pharmacy, the German Drugstore, and Products from the Homeland

Moving forced me to reconsider products I had treated as permanent. Instead of asking, “Where can I find this exact item?” I started asking, “What job is this product doing in my routine?” That question makes substitution much easier.

If the job is gentle cleansing, I do not necessarily need one American cleanser. I need a cleanser with the right texture, surfactants, and finish for my skin. If the job is barrier support, I can compare formulas instead of brand familiarity. If the job is sunscreen, I can take advantage of the fact that European sunscreen options are often superior and widely available.

French pharmacies gave me access to incredible formulas and brands that are not always easy or affordable to find in the United States. Their practical, effective approach suits me. There is something satisfying about buying a product designed to calm, protect, or repair skin without being told it contains diamonds and moonlight.

German drugstores brought affordable basics. Germany is very good at the unglamorous product that quietly does its job. Not every bottle should be the star; sometimes I want a gentle wash or dependable body sunscreen I can use generously without calculating the cost of every pump.

Because I visit family in Poland, I have also discovered thoughtful Polish brands that receive little attention in English-language skincare content, often at refreshingly sane prices. I am still learning, which is part of the fun, and I want this blog to leave room for those discoveries.

My European routine is therefore not just a compromised version of my American routine. In several categories, it is an upgrade. I lost convenient access to some favorites, found acceptable replacements for others, and discovered products I might never have tried if my old routine had remained effortless to repurchase.

It was not all that bad. In fact, it was mostly very good—after I stopped being annoyed.

## What Has Stayed the Same

For all this talk about evolution, the most important parts of my routine are remarkably stubborn.

First, I remain consistent. Products need time to work, and your skin may need time to adjust. Some active ingredients can cause an initial adjustment period, while irritation or a true reaction is a reason to stop and reassess. The answer is not to force your skin through pain because someone online called it “purging.” Pay attention, introduce new products thoughtfully, and seek professional advice when something is wrong.

Second, I keep using the products and tools that have earned my trust. I enjoy testing something new, but novelty is not a skincare benefit. If an existing product works beautifully, fits my budget, and remains available, it does not need to be replaced because a prettier bottle appeared on Instagram.

Third—and if you remember only one thing from this entire post, make it this—I wear SPF every day.

Every day means winter, clouds, staying home near windows, and days when applying sunscreen feels unnecessarily optimistic. If I am outside, swimming, or sweating, I reapply. I use hats, shade, umbrellas, and sun-protective clothing when appropriate. When I rent a car somewhere sunny, I wear driving gloves because I attribute the sun spots on my left hand to years of driving in my teens and twenties without adequate sun protection. This may not be glamorous, but neither is trying to reverse years of preventable sun damage.

Many products are nice to have. Some provide a modest improvement. Some make the routine feel luxurious and enjoyable, which has value because enjoyment can support consistency. But no expensive serum can compensate for routinely leaving your skin unprotected from UV exposure.

SPF is the least negotiable part of my routine. Everything else is up for discussion.

## The New Version of an “Involved” Routine

My routine is still more involved than the average person’s. I have made peace with this. I enjoy the process, I care about the details, and I am willing to dedicate time to it. But “involved” means something different to me now.

It no longer means using the largest possible number of products. It means understanding which ingredients are already present, knowing when one sophisticated formula is more useful than four bottles, adjusting during pregnancy, and rebuilding slowly when circumstances change.

It also means accepting practical reality. A perfect product that I cannot reliably buy in Switzerland is not always perfect for my Swiss routine. A slightly less exciting replacement that is affordable, accessible, and consistently on my shelf may be the better choice. On the other hand, if nothing else performs like a US favorite, I will continue sending my visitors a shopping list. Balance. 😇

I have become less loyal to individual products and more loyal to what each step is meant to accomplish. That is what allows the routine to evolve without losing its structure.

## What I Hope This Blog Can Do

I do not want this blog to hand everyone the same eleven-step routine and send you shopping. I want it to help you understand what your skin needs, which steps will make the greatest difference, and where you can simplify without losing the benefits you care about.

Some of you will want the full routine. I support you. Some of you want three steps and a realistic budget. I support you too. The best routine is not the most impressive one; it is the one that addresses your needs and that you will actually follow.

I will share my current routine, the products that have survived every revision, the tools I continue to use, and the replacements I have found in Europe. I will also share alternatives for different budgets, locations, levels of dedication, skin types, and concerns. I want to explore French pharmacy favorites, excellent German drugstore basics, Polish discoveries, the American products still worth importing, and the products that are absolutely not worth paying international prices for.

I will explain why a product has a place instead of simply telling you to buy it. Ingredients matter. Formulation matters. The rest of the routine matters. Your skin matters. And sometimes the honest review will be that a product is lovely but completely unnecessary.

Above all, I will continue to emphasize consistency. Skin changes slowly. Trends change quickly. A routine needs enough stability for you to know what is working.

My own routine will keep evolving. I expect products to improve. I expect my skin to change as I age. Seasons, hormones, motherhood, travel, and whatever country I am shopping in will continue to affect what I use. I am not trying to preserve my 2018 routine in amber. I am trying to preserve what made it effective: attention, intention, and the willingness to do it every day.

The routine is simpler now, but it is not less thoughtful. It contains fewer single-ingredient serums, but the ingredients are still there. It makes room for motherhood without treating my needs as an afterthought. It has adapted to Europe and become more interesting because of it. It still includes trusted favorites, occasionally transported across the Atlantic by very patient people who love me.

The products change. The steps persist. And SPF remains non-negotiable.
